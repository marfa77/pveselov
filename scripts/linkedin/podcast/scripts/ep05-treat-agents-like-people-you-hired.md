# Ep 05 — Treat agents like people you hired
slug: treat-agents-like-people-you-hired

## Spoken

Welcome back. I am Pavel Veselov. This guide is for IT directors who are being asked about agents before the basics are in place.

If episode four was about redesigning work, episode five is about not handing that work a badge and a login without a manager. Phase four: govern agents like a workforce. Controls, ownership, and decision rights before autonomy.

Start with six controls before you grant autonomy. Governed data and memory access. Human in the loop for high-risk decisions. Testing with shut-off and rollback. Clear hand-off interfaces. A named owner for every agent. Ongoing security. Done when no agent acts autonomously in production without all six. BCG's Applied AI Index for twenty twenty six says about forty-two percent of companies expect to give agents real autonomy by twenty thirty, but only about five percent have all six controls today. Companies with all six report about three times the agentic value of those with one. Controls are not bureaucracy for its own sake. They are what make autonomy possible.

Give every agent a human owner and a lifecycle. Onboarding, evaluation, retraining, retirement — the way you manage staff. Done when there is an agent register with owner, scope, permissions, and last review date. MIT Sloan Management Review and BCG describe agentic AI as a tool that learns and a worker that is owned. Many respondents already see agents more like coworkers than tools. If that is the metaphor, then orphan agents are a management failure.

Share decision rights across business, HR, and IT. Business units own outcomes. HR owns how agents change roles and skills. IT owns platforms and security. They decide together. Done when agent decisions no longer sit with IT alone. HBR's authors and the MIT SMR–BCG work land in the same place: digital labour is governed jointly, or it is governed badly.

Give whoever runs AI real authority. Whether that is you as CIO or an AI lead under you, the role needs budget, decision rights over the portfolio, and a CEO-level sponsor. Done when the owner of AI can stop a project and move its budget without begging line by line. Gene Rapoport, one of the HBR authors, said it cleanly: if they just have a title, they end up being a mascot.

How this feels. Vendors will sell autonomy first. Your job is to sell a register, six controls, and a human name next to every agent. That is peer leadership, not fear.

This week.

Draft the six controls as a one-page gate. Nothing autonomous without a tick on all six.

Start an agent register — even if it has three rows. Owner, scope, last review.

Write who decides with you: one business name, one HR name, one IT name.

If you are the AI owner on paper only, book the conversation about budget and stop-rights with your sponsor.

Next: talk to finance in their language — outcomes, cost, retention, tokens.
