# Ep 10 — One company, start to finish
slug: one-company-start-to-finish

## Spoken

Hi. I am Pavel Veselov. This is the last episode of Building AI in the Enterprise — a warm guide for IT directors. Today we walk one company through the whole thing, so the pieces click together.

Meet a firm I will call North Harbour Trading. Mid-size. Several countries. A CFO who wants AI on the board pack. An IT director — that is you — with a normal budget and an abnormal number of Slack messages about copilots. Sales has a pilot. Finance has a chatbot nobody opens. Operations bought a vendor tool last year. People quietly use personal chat for drafting.

Month one, you diagnose. The spend map shows most AI money sitting in business units, just as BCG's Applied AI Index would lead you to expect. Shadow interviews show sales prefers a personal tool for proposal drafts because the official bot is slow. Retention on the big licence is weak by week four. You stop two pilots with no metric and no owner. You keep one sales workflow and one finance reporting assistant as candidates. The room is quieter already.

Month two, you pick fights. Domains: sales proposals and month-end commentary. Both text-heavy, reviewable, verifiable enough for wave one. Operations automation waits — too many systems, absorb risk high, like the HBR warning on designs that factories cannot use. Buy for the commodity drafting layer. Build the thin workflow that applies your firm's judgment and templates. You redesign proposal drafting with two senior sellers. Judgment goes into context files. Architecture stays a model call plus retrieval, not an unsupervised agent. People budget includes seller training time.

Month three, you govern and measure. Anything that might send mail or change a record needs the six controls and a named owner. Finance owns the outcome metrics: proposal cycle time and win-rate movement against baseline; cost per completed draft including human edit time — Ewing's lesson sits on the dashboard. Retention is reported honestly; nobody mandates the tool. Token ceilings have owners. When a vendor pushes company-wide rollout, you run the scale gate. Cycle time is better. Cost per draft is still above manual for messy deals. You scale only the clean segment, keep the rest in learning. One program. One review rhythm. You are no longer collecting pilots like stamps.

What North Harbour did not do. They did not wait for perfect research agreement. They used episode eight's positions: measure their own baselines; buy commodity, build judgment; controls before autonomy. They sent people to pveselov.space slash ai when a step needed the done-when and the source.

What I want for you after these ten episodes. You can explain the job without jargon. You can diagnose without a war. You can pick a few fights. You can redesign work with the people who live it. You can refuse orphan agents. You can talk to finance without vanity metrics. You can scale only what survives. And when the experts disagree, you have a place to stand.

Keep the written playbook open. It stays current when research moves. This podcast was the guide. The site is the desk reference.

If this helped, share it with another IT director who just got asked to do something with AI. Send them to pveselov.space slash ai for the checklist, and to pveselov.space slash ai slash podcast for the series.

Thank you for listening. I am Pavel Veselov. Build carefully. Measure honestly. Scale only what earns it.
