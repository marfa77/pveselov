# Ep 02 — Look before you buy
slug: look-before-you-buy

## Spoken

Welcome back. I am Pavel Veselov. This is Building AI in the Enterprise — a practical guide for IT directors.

Episode one gave you the map. Today we do the first real work: look before you buy. In the playbook this is phase one — diagnose before you build. In plain language: for about thirty days, find what the company already pays for, uses, and quietly abandons, before you propose anything new.

I know the pressure. A vendor demo went well. A peer in another firm just announced a copilot. Your sponsor wants a date. Buying feels like progress. Looking feels like delay. It is not. Looking is how you stop paying twice for the same unfinished idea.

Start with spend. Pull AI-related invoices, cloud and model bills, SaaS add-ons, and business-unit contracts into one view. Give every line a named owner and a stated business purpose. Done when you can say that out loud without hedging. BCG's Applied AI Index for twenty twenty six is why this matters: about eighty percent of AI spend sits outside the enterprise IT budget. Your ledger is not the company's ledger until you merge them.

Next, shadow AI. Find which personal tools people already use, for which tasks, and why they prefer them to the official ones. Done when you can name the top five unofficial use cases and the official alternative for each — or the honest gap. MIT's GenAI Divide report found personal AI use above ninety percent in surveyed companies, with official subscriptions far behind. One lawyer in that research kept using ChatGPT instead of her firm's expensive tool because the output was better. If you buy without mapping shadow use, you will fund a shelf.

Then run a retention curve on your three biggest AI licences. Group users by the month they activated. Check how many are still active at week one, week four, and week twelve. Tag each contract keep, fix, or cut well before renewal. A steep drop in week one usually means onboarding or role fit. A slow bleed to week twelve usually means workflow friction. Define active user yourself, and ask for event-level usage exports. Otherwise you are reading the vendor's definition of success. The idea comes from the CIO.com line of work on measuring abandonment, not only adoption — Nishant Virmani's column is in the playbook library.

Last part of diagnose: inventory every live pilot. Sponsor, business metric, cost. Stop the ones with no outcome metric or no owner. Done when only a handful remain, each tied to a real business outcome. In Harvard Business Review's manufacturer case, fourteen workshop use cases became two rebuilt workflows — and much of the value came from dropping the other twelve. BCG's AI lead Nicolas de Bellefonds put it bluntly: if you see a roadmap with two hundred use cases, walk away.

How to do this without a war. You are not accusing people of waste. You are offering clarity. Sit with finance on the spend map. Sit with a few frontline people on shadow tools — ask what they actually open on Monday morning. Sit with sponsors on pilots: keep, fix, or stop, with a date.
