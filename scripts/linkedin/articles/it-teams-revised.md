# How IT Teams in Commodity Trading Evolve — And What Changes with AI

Key takeaways:

- Deloitte's 2024 Global Outsourcing Survey: 70% of organizations brought part of their outsourced scope back in-house in the past five years
- Domain knowledge — CTRM, operations, data — cannot be safely contracted out in trading
- Insource what needs your firm's context; buy what can be specified without it
- AI reduces the RUN burden; it amplifies the value of people who understand the business

[CONFIRM] As Head of IT at a trading group, one question kept coming back from the COO and CFO: how should we structure IT, and how much should we keep in-house versus contract out?

The answer is not obvious.

## What the research supports

Deloitte's 2024 Global Outsourcing Survey of more than 500 executives found that 70% of organizations had selectively brought part of their previously outsourced scope back in-house in the past five years. Most did it very selectively. The leading drivers: better control over service quality and performance (68%), growing strategic capabilities in-house (64%) and spend optimization (56%).

This is not a retreat from outsourcing. Deloitte's point is rebalancing: keep in-house what builds capability, buy what doesn't.

[CONFIRM] In trading I have seen the same arc. Smaller firms outsource heavily because they have to: they don't have the headcount to cover everything. As they mature, the work that needs the firm's own context moves in-house.

## Why commodity trading is different

In most industries, the insource/outsource decision is primarily about cost and capability. In commodity trading, there is a third variable that changes the calculus entirely: domain knowledge.

A CTRM administrator who understands how your firm structures deals — the pricing formulas, the exception handling, the way vessel nominations flow into settlements — cannot be replaced by a contractor who has never sat next to a trader. A business analyst who bridges commercial operations and IT, who recognizes when a "minor data quality issue" is actually a risk exposure problem, has to be yours. The IT manager who understands that a system outage during Asian market hours is not equivalent to a Sunday afternoon outage — that judgment comes from proximity to the business over years, not from an SLA.

IT unavailability in a live trading environment is not an inconvenience. It costs money while positions are open. The institutional knowledge that prevents those incidents cannot be contracted out.

## What you can safely outsource

The principle is not "keep expensive roles in-house and outsource cheap ones." It is: insource what requires your firm's context to do well; outsource what can be clearly specified without it.

Infrastructure management, first-line helpdesk, security operations, and project-based development work fall into the second category. A well-governed cloud environment does not require a dedicated internal cloud engineer at a 300-person trading firm. Desktop support can be managed regionally by a provider with clear SLAs. These functions are commodity work — the market delivers them well at competitive cost.

CTRM administration, data architecture, business analysis bridging commercial and IT, and IT leadership itself belong to the first category. These are the roles where outsourcing consistently underdelivers in trading environments, not because contractors are less skilled, but because the work requires context that takes years to build.

## How AI is shifting the equation

Deloitte sees AI and automation — the "digital workforce" — emerging as a sourcing model in its own right. Yet only 20% of the organizations it surveyed are developing a strategy for it, and fewer than half of those using AI in outsourced services report productivity gains. Buying AI through a vendor does not, by itself, deliver the gain.

In commodity trading, the functions that consume the most IT time are structural and repetitive: settlements reconciliation, document processing, incident triage, monitoring. These are exactly the areas where AI agents can take a real share of the work.

But here is what does not change: the value of the person who knows which processes to automate, how to validate the outputs, and when the system is giving you a number that looks right but is not. AI amplifies domain expertise. It does not replace it.

The business analyst who understands your freight procurement well enough to design an AI agent that catches demurrage discrepancies before they become claims is more valuable in 2026 than they were in 2020. The IT leader who can separate AI applications ready for production from those that are still vendor marketing is a business-critical hire.

## The model that works

[CONFIRM] For a commodity trading firm at $500M to $2B in annual volume, the core to keep in-house is IT leadership, CTRM and business applications, data and integration, and infrastructure oversight — in my experience a team of roughly 6–12 people. Contractors handle specialist and execution-heavy work.

The firms that get this right are not the ones with the largest IT teams or the most aggressive outsourcing contracts. They are the ones that have clearly identified which functions require institutional knowledge, protected those in-house, and used the rest of their budget — and increasingly their AI tooling — to keep the lights on efficiently.

The question is not how many IT people you need. It is which ones you cannot afford to not have on your own payroll.

The author ran IT for an international commodity trading group in Dubai with teams across six countries, after nearly a decade delivering SAP programmes as a consultant.
